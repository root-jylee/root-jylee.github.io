Research

What we study, and why we start at the physical layer

Overview

Most security work happens above the physical layer, with keys, certificates, and protocols. We work one level down. A radio signal carries more than its payload. The channel it crossed, the hardware that produced it, and where and how fast the transmitter was moving all leave marks on what the receiver sees. Those marks are hard to forge and hard to erase, and that is what makes them useful.

Our research asks how far this can be pushed. Can the marks hide a transmission altogether? Can they prove who sent a packet? Can two radios turn them into a key that nobody else holds? Can the noise and mixing of signals in the air keep a user’s data private? Each of these is a security objective, and each one ends up as a question about how the communication system itself should be built.

01 · Existence

Covert Communication

Encryption hides what is being said. Covert communication hides that anything is being said at all. A warden watching the band should not be able to tell a transmission from background noise, while the intended receiver still decodes reliably. The two demands pull against each other, and the physics decides how much room there is between them.

Most of our work here is about getting that room from the network rather than from a single transmitter. When several users share a band, their signals and the uncertainty about who is active can cover for each other. We have looked at how much covert throughput this buys, when channel correlation helps and when it hurts, and how to pick the cooperating set without any pre-shared secret. More recently we have been asking what changes when the transmitter can also sense the warden and adapt, which is where our covert ISAC work is heading.

What we measure, what we tune

Covertness is measured by the warden’s detection error probability and the covert rate. The design knobs are power allocation, relay placement, RIS phases, and user selection.

02 · Authenticity

Physical-Layer Authentication

A certificate proves that someone holds a key. It says nothing about whether the radio in front of you is the one it claims to be. Physical-layer authentication looks at the signal itself. Oscillator offsets, amplifier nonlinearity, the multipath of a particular link, and the Doppler and delay that a moving platform must show all leave a fingerprint that a spoofer standing somewhere else cannot easily reproduce.

We are especially interested in platforms that move: drones, ships, satellites, and underwater vehicles. Motion is usually treated as a nuisance, but for authentication it is an asset, because a legitimate transmitter has to show a trajectory-consistent signature and an impostor has to fake one. Our single-packet scheme for UAVs uses a spatial challenge and response so that a drone can be checked from one packet. We are extending the same idea to LEO satellite passes, where the orbit itself becomes the challenge. On the network side, we are working out what happens to secrecy in a cell-free massive MIMO system when access points can only be authenticated with some probability, and how the secrecy outage depends on that.

Under water the same idea takes a different form. Acoustic channels are sparse and strongly tied to location, so we estimate the channel with compressive sensing, compress the estimate with a conditional variational autoencoder, and let the receiver check whether an unmanned underwater vehicle really answered from the zone it was asked to move to. No fingerprint map has to be stored, which matters on a node with little memory and power.

What we measure, what we tune

Performance is measured by false-alarm and missed-detection probabilities. The design knobs are which features to use, how to set thresholds, and how to shape the challenge.

03 · Secret Key

Physical-Layer Key Generation

Two radios that talk over the same channel see nearly the same fading, while a third radio a few wavelengths away sees something different. That asymmetry is enough to build a key. Both ends measure the channel, quantize it, reconcile the few bits that disagree, and end up with a shared secret that never went over the air.

How fast this works depends on how quickly the channel changes and how well the two measurements match, which makes it a system design question rather than a coding one. For a drone the altitude and speed set the rate. For a ground link a reconfigurable intelligent surface can stir the channel on purpose. Our first project here combines an RIS with artificial noise, so that the legitimate pair sees a rich, fast-changing channel while an eavesdropper’s view of it stays uninformative. This is a newer line for us, and the natural first step toward the quantum key distribution we would like to reach eventually.

What we measure, what we tune

We track the key generation rate and the key disagreement rate. The design knobs are altitude and speed, RIS use, probing rate, and frequency band.

04 · Data Privacy

Physical-Layer-Aided Differential Privacy

When many devices send model updates to a server, the server, or anyone listening, can sometimes infer things about a single participant. Differential privacy bounds that inference by adding noise. Our observation is that a wireless channel already adds noise, and over-the-air aggregation already mixes the signals, so part of the privacy budget can come from the physics instead of from noise added in software.

The design problem is to control that noise and mixing, through power control, scheduling, beamforming, and artificial noise where needed, so that the privacy guarantee holds and the learned model is still useful. Our first concrete design, DPRIMO-FL, does this for MIMO over-the-air federated learning: the base station’s antennas and the users’ transmit powers are set together so that the aggregate the server receives is differentially private by construction. This line connects directly to the distributed AI networks in our NRF project.

What we measure, what we tune

Privacy is measured as an (ε, δ) guarantee against learning utility. The design knobs are power control, beamforming, user scheduling, artificial noise, and over-the-air aggregation.
